The Margin Nobody Envies

October 22, 2026

In most industries, a 25 percent profit margin is considered strong and healthy. In childcare, the commonly cited range is 8 to 15 percent, and some federal analysis puts the typical center closer to 1 percent, and the honest truth is that many centers are not profitable at all. Many owners I know do not pay themselves, and almost all of them work more than 40 hours a week.

On paper, our model can reach an 18 to 20 percent margin, and I want to be honest that this number is unrealized. It assumes a full school for the full year, and a partially full school often does not break even, because the costs of running the building do not shrink when a classroom has empty seats. Labor and rent are the two biggest line items, and for us labor alone runs about 100 thousand dollars a month, with rent close to 30 thousand, and that is not even a school at full capacity. Expenses also creep in ways no model fully predicts, because staffing always costs more than planned and building costs and supplies only go up.

So where does the margin go? For us, it goes to choices. Our lead teachers, infant teachers included, get almost eight weeks of paid time off, and our assistants, get almost six. We offer medical, dental, vision, a 401k, and a childcare benefit, with some staff receiving free childcare and others 50 to 75 percent off. Assistant teachers in the Austin area often make 14 to 16 dollars an hour, and we generally start at 17 or more depending on experience, and when a lead interviews we ask what they make now and always try to either meet it or exceed it. We also try to staff a floater for every age group on top of the lead and assistant in each classroom.

We have been told many times to cut the benefits, pay less, and raise prices. In four years we have raised tuition by 35 dollars, and our pricing is all inclusive, covering food, supplies, materials, and outings, because we wanted clear, transparent pricing.

The margin also comes out of the founders. Shavonda and I keep our salaries intentionally low with the understanding that we are building something, and even those salaries do not always get paid. On October 10th of this year, I received my first paycheck in a year and a half, and it was 1,200 dollars. I am not sharing that for sympathy, because this was a choice we made with our eyes open. I am sharing it because when people ask why childcare costs so much while childcare workers earn so little and centers still struggle, the answer is sitting right there, and somebody has to absorb the gap.

So why stay? Because I love children, I love Montessori learning, and I genuinely believe there is something here that could change the world. Some of that belief comes from my own story. I did not grow up in the easiest environment, and both inside and outside my home I have seen some ugly sides of humanity. I know firsthand what a child's environment does to the person they become, and that is exactly why I believe so deeply in building better ones. We do this work because we want to make a difference, and we expect the same of everyone we hire, because honestly, that is the only way anyone lasts in this field.

Montessori education is humanitarian education, and I believe we need environments that push children to be more empathic, connected, accountable, and emotionally mature, and to think creatively and critically. And this field is hard, though not for the reasons most people assume. The children are the least difficult part of this work. They are lovely even when they are not, and they are the easiest even when they are hard. The heavier weight comes from everything around them, and if I am being fully honest, the most challenging part of this job is often the adults. That deserves its own conversation, and it is one I plan to have here soon.

Whenever it does not seem worth it, that is the belief that will not let me quit, so I keep going.
