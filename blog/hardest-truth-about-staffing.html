The Hardest Truth About Childcare Staffing

November 2, 2026

When a teacher leaves a school unexpectedly, parents feel it personally, and I understand why completely. To a parent, this person was not an employee, she was the caregiver of their child, the one who knew that he naps on his left side and that she needs her banana sliced a very particular way. When that person is suddenly gone, parents are hurt, sometimes offended, and they want answers from the school.

So I want to offer the honest explanation, with love for everyone in it.

The people who care for your children are, at the end of the day, hourly employees in one of the lowest paid professions in the country. In our schools, assistant teachers typically earn somewhere between 18 and 22 dollars an hour, and we work hard to sit at the top of that range, but the range itself is the reality of an industry where the money simply is not there, for reasons I have written about at length. We search hard for the best people we can find, we invest in them, we hope they stay, and we hope they fall in love with this work and want to train toward becoming a lead one day. Many do, and those people are the soul of our school.

And some discover that working with children is not their long term path, which is a completely human thing to discover. Caring for other people's children is beautiful, exhausting, underpaid work, and not everyone who tries it is meant to stay in it forever. When someone leaves, it does not necessarily mean the school failed them, or that they failed, or that anyone did anyone wrong. Sometimes a person simply did their best at a hard job for the season they were in it, and then their life moved in another direction.

What I can promise is what we control. We impart to every person we hire that they are working with the thing parents cherish most in the entire world, and we hold that standard seriously. We pay what the model allows and then some, we train, we credential, and we build the kind of workplace people want to stay in, with benefits this industry rarely offers. And when someone does leave, we focus on the only thing that actually protects your child's experience: the consistency of the environment, the systems, and the community around every classroom, so that no single departure can take the school's heart with it.

Your child's teacher deserves your warmth, and she also deserves some grace, because she is doing her absolute best at work that matters enormously and pays modestly. We give our people that grace. I hope this helps explain why we ask families to extend it too.

And here is the part my clinical background will not let me leave out. Your children are okay. Teachers are imperfect people, and children are profoundly resilient, and those two facts were designed to coexist. I hear and feel the heartbreak underneath so many parent worries, which is really the grief of not being able to be with your child all day and the weight of having to trust someone else with them. That is real, and it deserves to be named. But what decades of child development research keeps confirming is that children do not need perfect adults. They need consistent, caring environments and a team of adults who are on the same side, and when the parents and the school genuinely operate as partners, that is the environment where children thrive best.

So this is my invitation. Our door is open. Come see what your children are doing all day, ask us anything, bring us your feedback and your curiosities, and get to know the people who spend their days with the people you love most. Our hearts are always in the right place, even when we do not always show up perfectly, and your grace for our teachers, like our grace for them, is really just love for your child wearing its work clothes.
