Your Child Is More Resilient Than You Think

November 18, 2026

Before I was a school founder, before I was a CFO, I spent years in clinical social work. I worked in hospice, in trauma, in addiction counseling. I have sat with people in the hardest moments of their lives, and I have sat with people at the very end of their lives. I bring all of that with me when I talk to a worried parent, because almost every fear I hear from parents is one I understand, and almost every one of them is heavier than it needs to be. It is also part of why I chose Montessori for my own children before I ever helped build a school, because Montessori is one of the few approaches to education built on more than a century of developmental research and observation, and everything I had learned clinically about how children actually work, I found already woven into how Montessori classrooms are run.

Here is the fear underneath most of the calls and emails we get. Parents believe that if anything hard happens to their child at school, a conflict with another child, a fall on the playground, a teacher who leaves, a rough dropoff, their child will be left scarred with lasting impacts. I want to speak to that fear directly, because I have the training to say this with confidence: that is not how children work.

I say that with more than theory behind it. Part of my clinical career was spent working with children who had witnessed domestic violence between their parents, and some who were victims of it directly. These were children carrying experiences no child should carry, and I watched them recover, adapt, and grow. What that work taught me is that children are profoundly resilient, and that resilience holds unless the adults around them interfere with it and injure it. The human race has survived famine, war, and every hardship history could throw at it, and children have always been part of that survival. Your child falling on the playground or losing a toy dispute is not going to break something that millennia could not.

The Safety Paradox

Let me start with the one that surprises parents the most. When children feel safe with a person or an environment, they show that person their worst behavior and their biggest emotions. When children do not feel safe, they often appear better behaved and more polite. This is why so many parents see the hardest side of their child at home and then feel a wave of relief, or sometimes confusion, when they hear their child doesn't act that way at school or at a friend's house. Your child saving their meltdowns for you is not a sign that something is wrong. It is a sign that you are their safest place.

This is also why it is so normal for a child to come home and tell mom or dad how terrible school was. They are doing two things at once, feeling safe enough to express themselves with you, and testing boundaries to see how you react. And your reaction teaches them something valuable either way. If a child learns that saying the right dramatic thing gets a big reaction, or better yet gets them out of school tomorrow, that lesson reinforces itself, because children are brilliant students of their parents. Now, if something is actually happening to your child and they are not safe, of course you should respond, and respond appropriately. But for the normal situations, a child saying I don't like school, or my teacher is mean, or that kid hits me every day, that does not mean your child is having a negative experience all day long. Young children are still learning impulse control, and hitting, pushing, and grabbing are developmentally normal behaviors that every child does and every child experiences at some point, which is exactly why classrooms exist, to practice better tools. What it means when your child tells you about it is that your child feels safe with you, and that they either know or are busy learning exactly where your reactions and boundaries are.

The same logic applies in reverse. A nurturing classroom will see big emotions, because children in environments where they feel secure are busy doing the real work of childhood, which is testing boundaries and getting in touch with feelings they do not yet know how to manage. This is actually why Montessori calls the classroom a prepared environment. It is deliberately designed, down to the furniture and the materials, so that children can do that work safely, with real choices, real consequences that are small enough to learn from, and trained adults observing rather than hovering.

Good Enough Is Actually the Goal

There is a concept from the psychoanalyst Donald Winnicott called the good enough mother, and it is one of the most freeing ideas in all of child development. The research has held up for decades. Children do not need perfect parents or perfect schools. They need good enough ones, environments that are warm, responsive, and safe, and that also allow for ordinary frustration, ordinary conflict, and ordinary disappointment. A good enough environment raises a happy, well adjusted child. A perfect, friction free environment does not exist, and chasing it actually works against the child.

Because here is the part we don't like to hear. Children have better outcomes when they struggle. When a child has to figure out how to resolve a disagreement, how to wait, how to recover from a hard morning, they are being forced, in the best sense, to think critically about how to help themselves. When adults step in and smooth every moment, we rob children of exactly the experiences that build empathy and critical thought. Montessori understood this over a hundred years ago. It is why our guides do not rush to fix every struggle, why children carry real glass pitchers and work with real tools, and why the method trusts children with independence that surprises many first time visitors. None of that is carelessness. It is the opposite, it is a researched, humanitarian approach to education that treats struggle as the raw material of development.

I will be honest, I worry about the long term effects of the helicopter parenting I see now. The children who are most protected from difficulty tend to have the lowest resilience, and they also tend to become skilled at managing their parents' reactions, because children are extraordinarily intelligent and they learn very early how to get a response from the adults around them. That is not a character flaw in the child. It is part of the intelligent design of childhood. But it means the adults have to be thoughtful about what we are teaching.

And I want to be clear that the stakes here are not abstract. Competence is where confidence comes from. A young person who has never been allowed to struggle, never been required to do hard things, grows up feeling inept, and a person who feels inept loses faith in their own ability to make something of their life. We are seeing the consequences of that in the mental health of teenagers and young adults right now, and it is one of the reasons I care so much about letting children practice difficulty while the stakes are still small.

Empathy works the same way. Children are not born with it. Empathy is taught, modeled, and practiced, and the classroom is one of the richest places to practice it, precisely because your child will share a room with children who are different from them, who are still learning social cues, who are at different stages of managing their bodies and their feelings. Those moments of friction are not the system failing. They are the curriculum. A child who never encounters a classmate having a hard time never gets the chance to learn compassion for one.

This is also where I want to say something about the people doing this work. Our guides have spent years in training to earn their Montessori credentials, learning how to observe children, how to navigate every one of these developmentally appropriate interactions and behaviors, and how to recognize and support the things that are developmentally unusual or atypical as well. When your child has a conflict at school, there is not a random adult shrugging nearby. There is a professional who studied for years to know exactly what that moment is, what it is teaching, and when it needs intervention versus when it needs space.

What Real Distress Actually Looks Like

Real distress usually looks quieter than parents expect. It is a child who shuts down, goes passive, and loses interest in play. It is play that turns repetitive, the same troubling scene acted out over and over without resolution, which is something I learned to read during my years using play therapy. It is recurring nightmares, new fears that stick around, and a pattern that persists over weeks rather than one hard afternoon. I want to be careful with one sign you may have read about, which is regression, a child losing skills they had already mastered. Regression on its own is usually not a red flag at all, because children commonly regress around ordinary transitions, a new classroom, a new sibling, a family vacation, a move. That is a normal response to change, and it resolves on its own. Regression matters when it arrives alongside the rest of the picture, the shutdown, the nightmares, the repetitive play, and when it does not lift as the child settles. And here is the reassuring part: real distress is not very common in caring, nurturing environments, because those environments are where children learn to regulate in the first place.

What you will see often is a child who appears to be in complete crisis because of the way you cut their sandwich. It looks real, and to the child it is real, because children have not yet developed a gauge for their emotions. Every feeling arrives at full volume. The crying over the sandwich is not a problem to prevent. It is the practice field. If we arrange a child's life so that nothing ever upsets them, they never learn to regulate at all, and I would ask you to imagine managing a workplace of forty adults who never learned to regulate their emotions. Some of you do not have to imagine.

What Hospice Taught Me About What Matters

When I sat with people at the end of their lives, they did not talk about the minute failings of the people who loved them. They talked about relationships. Over and over, I heard people say they wished they had not taken so many small things so seriously, that they wished they had let more go. Nobody at the end of life is cataloging the bad dropoffs. I try to let that perspective size my worries as a parent, and I offer it to you for the same purpose.

What to Do Instead of Worrying

So what do I want a worried parent to actually do, the next time something at school rattles you? First, think of your child as resilient, because they are, and think of every ordinary hard moment as an opportunity for them to learn how to interact with others, discover their own boundaries, and practice regulating their emotions. Then help them do that work. Name the emotion with them, give it a word, let them talk it through.

And as your child gets older, remember that children will vent to their parents, and venting does not always mean they want mama bear to go in and handle it. Sometimes they just need a safe space to work something out, a non reactive adult who will sit with them judgment free and hold that space while they feel what they feel, and maybe, when they are ready, a little advice on how they might handle the next situation differently. That is the parent children remember. Not the one who prevented every hard thing, but the one who sat with them while they learned they could survive it.
